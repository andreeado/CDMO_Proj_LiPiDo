This approach is more efficient, as it avoids function calls and uses direct array lookup, which is

2.2.5 Implied Defectiveness Constraint
To justify this constraint we need a preparatory result.
There exists a surjective function δ : [n] → [P ] assigning each team to a unique defective period,
namely a period in which the team appears only once. Each period’s preimage contains exactly two
teams (it has exactly two teams that map to it via δ).
Fix a period p ∈ [P ]. Each period contains W = n − 1 matches, and therefore 2(n − 1) total team
appearances.
By constraint 2.2.4, each team can appear in any period at most twice. So the maximum number
of appearances across all n teams in a period is 2n. Since only 2(n − 1) slots are used, exactly two
team appearances are missing.
Suppose both missing appearances belonged to the same team t. Then t plays 0 times in period p,
and must play all n − 1 matches in the remaining P − 1 periods. But since each team appears at most
twice per period, this gives at most 2(P − 1) = 2(n/2 − 1) = n − 2 appearances, which is insufficient.
Therefore, the missing appearances must belong to distinct teams, each appearing exactly once in
p. We call such teams defective in period p.
Since there are n/2 periods and each team is defective in exactly one of them (to satisfy 2.2.2), the
mapping:
δ : Team 7 → Defective Period
is a surjective function, and each codomain pre-image contains exactly two teams.
The constraint we thus enforce is tied with the functional property: for each team there exists
exactly one period in which it is defective. A stronger constraint could be constructed by symmetry
breaking and fixing exactly which teams are defective in which period, but this constraint conflicts
with some symmetry breaking specified in section XX that proves to be computationally faster.
2.2.6 Symmetry Breaking Constraints
2.2.7 Ordered vs. Unordered Matches
The solution of the problem and the optimisation of its objective functions are two completely inde-
pendent tasks, only connected by their shared parameter n. This is because we know a priori that
the schedule will contain all possible matches (as 2 elements subsets of the set of all teams) and the
home or away state of a match does not interact with when it will occur (as it would have, say, if we
imposed that no team plays more than k matches in a row at home).
This also means that, with a fixed n, once that an optimally balanced home/away match spread
is obtained, it can be overlayed on any solution of the CP problem with no additional cost, making
it optimal. Conversely, the construction of the problem solution has no bearing on optimality and all
solutions have the chance to become optimal. This consideration allows us to break down the problem
in two parts, one agnostic to home/away distinctions and the other agnostic to scheduling, that only
interact in the output section of the MiniZinc script.
If matches were modeled as ordered pairs (i.e., including home/away status), the solution space
would increase by a factor of 2M = 2n(n−1)/2. By decoupling optimization (COP) from the solution
structure (CP), we avoid this exponential blowup, letting the two complexities combine additively and
not multiplicatively.
2.2.8 Invariances in Solution space
The search space exhibits substantial symmetry. Every valid solution is invariant under the following
operations:
• Permutations of the weeks (columns of the schedule matrix),
• Permutations of the periods (rows of the schedule matrix),
• Permutations of the team labels.

Result: Symmetry Structure and Canonical Representation Every feasible solution remains
invariant under the action of a group isomorphic to:
Sn/2 × Sn−1 × Sn (8)
• Sn/2 acts by permuting the rows (periods),
• Sn−1 acts by permuting the columns (weeks),
• Sn acts by relabelling the teams.
Each of these permutation groups operates independently, and their actions commute pairwise,
hence the direct product property. Specifically:
• Row permutations correspond to right-multiplication of the schedule matrix by permutation
matrices.
• Column permutations correspond to left-multiplication.
• Label permutations act uniformly on team identifiers, replacing each team with another through-
out the entire schedule.
Since permutation groups are generated by transpositions, it suffices to verify commutativity on
transpositions. This guarantees full closure of the symmetry group under the group product structure
given by the direct product specified above.

If the entire representation class of a solution matrix is to be examined, each team becomes sim-

symbols—i.e., a bijective assignment of natural numbers 1, . . . , n to the teams.
To fix this symmetry aspect, we begin by designating one team to receive the label 1. This
introduces n possible choices. By the previously established structural lemma, team 1 is defective in
exactly one period, meaning it appears only once in that period. Within this defective period, there
is a unique match in which it participates, and that match occurs in a specific week. We now isolate
this week and use it as a basis to fix the remaining team labels.
During this designated week, there are n/2 matches involving all n teams. We assign labels such
that each match corresponds to a pair 2k − 1, 2k for k = 1, . . . , n/2, with team 1 playing against
team 2. There are (n/2 − 1)! ways to assign the remaining teams to these n/2 − 1 matches, and
for each match, two options depending on which team receives the smaller label. Thus, we obtain a
multiplicative factor of 2n/2−1 from these binary label choices. These add up to the n = 2 ∗ n/2 choices

2.3 Validation
The algorithm above is tested experimentally in four different varieties:
a) A fragmented implementation, in which the two searches are executed in different files, with the
benefit of an independent choice of search annotations in the two searches.
b) A complete implementation, in which the same MiniZinc File is responsible for both the constraint
satisfaction and the optimization part of the problem, with the symmetry breaks previously
discussed
c) A complete implementation with no symmetry breaks on the order of rows and columns.
d) A complete implementation in which the symmetry breaking technique of decoupling the two
searches was omitted.
The reported results are for the hyperparamenter n = 14, the largest for which the majority of
searches terminated successfully. The results shown are repetitions of the search over different random
seeds, since the searches use a random assignment of variables among the values not pruned away by
constraints. For all methods, the search consists of two non-interacting components: the integer search
for the IDs of the match to be played and the boolean search for the array of swap variables.
The primary phase utilizes int search on the match vars decision variables with a first fail
variable ordering heuristic, which prioritizes variables with the smallest remaining domain size to
exploit the fail-first principle and achieve early pruning of infeasible branches in the search tree. The
indomain random value selection strategy introduces stochastic diversification to escape local optima
and avoid deterministic cycling, particularly valuable given the highly symmetric nature of tournament
scheduling problems.
This secondary phase exhibits heavy-tail behavior characteristic of the exponential O(2n2
) search
space: while the majority (> 90%) of instances, even for n ≤ 20, terminate within 300ms, a patho-
logical subset fails to achieve termination within the full 300s timeout. The stochastic nature of
indomain random becomes crucial in mitigating these tail cases through restart mechanisms: the mere
presence of restart strategies proves sufficient to eliminate pathological behavior, with minimal perfor-
mance variation across different restart strategies.

However, solver-specific limitations constrain the implementation of optimal restart strategies. Cer-

Uniqueness of the canonical circle schedule. Let S be the set of all circle schedules (constructed
by the circle method) on teams {1, . . . , n}. Let G denote the group of schedule symmetries generated
by row permutations, column permutations and team relabellings (so in the unconstrained case G ∼=
Sn/2 ×Sn−1 ×Sn). Fix the canonicalization that (i) chooses team n as the central team and (ii) requires
that team n meets teams 1, 2, . . . , n − 1 in that order across the weeks. Call the resulting canonical
schedule C.
Theorem. The stabilizer of C in G is trivial; consequently, the canonicalization identifies every
circle schedule with a unique representative, i.e. all circle schedules collapse onto the single canonical
schedule C.
Proof. Let g ∈ G be any symmetry that fixes C. Write g = (ρ, κ, σ) corresponding respectively
to (possibly trivial) row permutation ρ ∈ Sn/2, column permutation κ ∈ Sn−1, and team relabelling
σ ∈ Sn. Because C has the central team preselected as n and the sequence of opponents of n fixed to
1, 2, . . . , n − 1, the following hold:
1. The requirement that team n remain the central team implies σ(n) = n. Any relabelling moving
n would move the centre and hence not fix C.
7
2. The requirement that n meets 1, 2, . . . , n − 1 in that exact order forces the induced permutation
of the outer cycle (the effect of κ together with the action of σ restricted to {1, . . . , n − 1}) to
be the identity on the ordered list (1, 2, . . . , n − 1). Thus the combined action on the outer cycle
is trivial.
3. Since the contents and order of the first week (and hence of every week and period in the circle
construction) are fixed by C, any row permutation ρ or column permutation κ that moves rows
or columns would change entries of C and therefore cannot fix C. Hence ρ and κ must both be
the identity permutations.
From (1)–(3) we conclude ρ = id, κ = id and σ fixes every element of {1, . . . , n}, so σ = id.
Therefore g is the identity element of G. Hence the stabilizer of C is trivial, and every circle schedule
is mapped by the canonicalization to the same unique representative C. □

From generic solutions to canonical circle schedules. In the unconstrained formulation of the
round-robin problem, every feasible schedule is invariant under

Circle schedules without period constraints. A circle schedule, in our relaxed sense, is defined
by the property that each week contains the correct set of team pairs; the exact assignment of matches
to periods (rows) is not fixed. Hence, any uniform permutation of periods across all weeks preserves
the circle property. Similarly, any relabeling of teams that preserves the central team structure also
yields a valid circle schedule. Thus, even in the circle-constrained formulation, there remains a residual
symmetry group generated by:
• uniform period permutations across all weeks,
• team relabelings consistent with the circle property,
• possible rotations/reflections of the outer (n − 1)-cycle.
Canonicalization via the first week. To remove the residual ambiguity due to uniform period
permutations, we fix the first week of the schedule to a canonical configuration:
∀p ∈ Periods : match vars[p, 1] = match ID(p, n + 1 − p).
This choice enforces a total order on the first column, breaking the period-permutation symmetry
while preserving the circle property in every week.
Canonical circle schedule. Finally, by selecting the central team (e.g., team n) and requiring
that it faces its opponents in the natural order 1, 2, . . . , n − 1, all remaining team-label and cycle
symmetries are eliminated. Consequently, the stabilizer of the canonical schedule is trivial, and every
circle schedule collapses onto a single representative.
Summary. The reduction of symmetry is therefore threefold:
Sn/2×Sn−1×Sn −→ (circle schedules with uniform period permutations) −→ {canonical circle schedule}.
This canonicalization drastically reduces redundancy while respecting the relaxed notion of circle
schedules.
